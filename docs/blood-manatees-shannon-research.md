# Blood Manatees: Dublin–Shannon–Miami research

Researched 20 September 2026. Supports Scenes 5–7. This is a fiction-development memo, not a carrier quote or a confirmed itinerary. No transport providers were contacted and nothing was booked.

## Chosen version

Ewen orders a driverless car from Dublin to Shannon, tries to buy a cargo flight for himself and his equipment, then learns that shipping the battery does not entitle him to ride with it. He pays a specialist to ship the battery and separately books a passenger connection to Miami. The bats travel as checked sports equipment, subject to airline acceptance. His objective survives, but his frictionless plan does not.

The departure city and e-foil purpose were confirmed by the author. The exact battery model, prices, transit times, personnel, recorded interfaces and successful freight acceptance are choices for this draft.

## The battery

Lift’s detailed support table lists the LIFTX battery at **0.9 kWh / 900 Wh and 16.8 lb**, approximately 7.6 kg. Its marketing FAQ describes the platform as **1 kWh**. The screenplay uses an unbranded 900 Wh pack, following the detailed specification rather than implying all e-foils use the same battery. For comparison, the LIFT5 full-range pack is 2.2 kWh. Battery generations are not automatically interchangeable. [Lift battery specifications](https://support.liftfoils.com/kb/batteries-overview), [Lift FAQ](https://liftfoils.com/pages/faq).

That makes the baggage problem substantial. Ordinary lithium-ion passenger batteries up to 100 Wh are generally permitted under applicable conditions; 101–160 Wh requires airline approval, while a recreational pack exceeding 160 Wh cannot be carried as ordinary passenger baggage. Spare batteries belong in carry-on, not checked luggage. Discharging a 900 Wh pack does not change its rated capacity or turn it into an eligible passenger battery. [FAA passenger battery guidance](https://www.faa.gov/hazmat/packsafe/airline-passengers-and-batteries).

A stand-alone rechargeable lithium-ion battery is treated as **UN 3480** for shipping. Under the standard air-cargo provisions, it travels on cargo aircraft rather than as freight aboard passenger aircraft, with a maximum 30% state of charge and the applicable packing, labeling and documentation requirements. A specialist must determine acceptance for the actual pack and route. The scene deliberately shows specialist assessment and repacking; a protective travel case alone does not authorize flight. [IATA shipping guidance](https://www.iata.org/en/publications/newsletters/iata-knowledge-hub/what-to-know-about-how-to-ship-lithium-batteries).

The manufacturer’s matching UN 38.3 test summary is a useful detail for Ewen to have ready, not a magic permit. DHL’s guidance distinguishes accessible test documentation from carrier acceptance and notes that not every battery shipment is accepted. [DHL documentation and acceptance checklist](https://www.dhl.com/discover/en-th/logistics-advice/essential-guides/lithium-batteries-dangerous-goods-checklist).

Used marine batteries are particularly important to disclose. Fliteboard directs owners to a dangerous-goods specialist, says its batteries cannot travel on passenger aircraft, and notes that its couriers cannot collect used/service batteries under its stated arrangements. This is **Fliteboard’s policy**, not proof that every other carrier refuses every used e-foil battery. Scene 7 invents an operator that accepts this undamaged pack after assessment; real acceptance could fail altogether. [Flitecell safety and shipping](https://support.fliteboard.com/hc/en-us/articles/360063241993-Flitecell-Safety-and-Shipping).

## The bats and the person

Bats are a cabin-security issue, rather than a reason to charter an aircraft. They can generally travel as suitably packed checked sporting equipment, with the actual airline’s size, weight and acceptance rules checked in advance. Aer Lingus identifies bats among restricted cabin articles, allows large sporting bats in the hold in its regional guidance, and provides for sports equipment within a transatlantic checked allowance or an additional purchased allowance. This is not a guarantee for an arbitrary bag on every connecting airline. [Aer Lingus restricted items](https://www.aerlingus.com/prepare/bags/restricted-items/), [Aer Lingus baggage information](https://www.aerlingus.com/localized/en/modals/baggage-information.html).

A cargo booking is not a retail passenger ticket. There are limited legitimate categories for people accompanying freight: for example, US Part 121 rules identify certain crew, employees and persons necessary for particular cargo duties. Those provisions depend on the operator and circumstances; owning a battery does not automatically qualify Ewen. They are not a blanket rule for every Irish or international operation. The agent’s “It isn’t for sale” is the fictional carrier’s answer, not a claim that nobody can ever travel on a freighter. [14 CFR 121.583](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-121/subpart-T/section-121.583).

## Shannon and the drive

Shannon has real cargo-handling operations, including Swissport. That supports the location, but does not establish a walk-in dangerous-goods booking service, a direct Shannon–Miami freighter or a seat Ewen can purchase. The freight desk, inspection workflow and dialogue are fictional; the scene leaves the actual freight routing unspecified and allows a road connection to another gateway. [Swissport Shannon](https://www.swissport.com/en/network/europe/ireland/snn).

Shannon’s published summer guide lists US passenger services including New York JFK, Newark, Boston and Chicago. It supports a US connection as a story choice; the draft’s particular Miami booking is invented and is not a checked through-ticket or live fare. [Shannon travel guide](https://travelguide.shannonairport.ie/view/634232944).

For the Dublin–Shannon trip, roughly 2.5–3 hours before substantial stops is a reasonable screenplay allowance. One transfer seller estimates 234 km and 2 hr 22 min between the airports; the draft starts at an unspecified Dublin apartment and includes a service stop. [Transfer route estimate](https://airportstaxitransfers.com/transportation/taxibookings/Transfer-from-Dublin-Airport-to-Shannon-Airport).

The **unattended cross-country robotaxi is near-future fiction**. FMCI provides a real autonomous-vehicle testing context in Shannon, but a proving ground is not evidence that this commercial door-to-door service currently exists. [FMCI proving ground](https://fmci.ie/proving-ground/).

## What I would do in real life

First, ask the e-foil manufacturer or a destination dealer about renting a **compatible** battery, or renting the complete e-foil, before moving a used pack internationally. Fliteboard explicitly suggests destination battery rental through its network; availability for Miami and compatibility with a Lift-based pack would still need confirmation. Buying a destination battery is another comparison, not automatically the cheapest answer: as an example of replacement cost, Flitecell Sport is listed at **US$2,860 before shipping and tax**, but it is not a compatible substitute for a LIFTX battery. [Fliteboard travel FAQ](https://fliteboard.com/pages/faqs), [Flitecell Sport price](https://fliteboard.com/products/fliteboard-sport-titanium-series-2-2).

If taking this exact battery is essential, arrange acceptance through the manufacturer and a qualified dangerous-goods forwarder **before leaving Dublin**. Provide the model, condition, test summary and requested route, obtain a written inclusive quote and collection arrangements, and let the specialist specify the packaging and shipping preparation. Then book a separate passenger itinerary, confirm the bats’ allowance across the connection, and put the eligible personal electronics in cabin baggage. Budget several days or longer for freight rather than planning a same-day walk-up. Sea freight is another specialist option where time permits. A real passenger trip also needs the normal entry documentation; no cargo arrangement substitutes for it.

## Screenplay choices and continuity

- Ewen’s documentation is prepared. His mistake is assuming that one correct set of data buys one seamless journey. This preserves his competence while exposing an area his workplace automation has not taught him to navigate.
- “I already own this one” carries the cost exposition through character. He is willing to spend heavily to preserve a plan that should have been reconsidered.
- Every image belongs to a named fixed camera. The wide road and exterior feeds have no audio. Dialogue comes from the fictional vehicle recorder and counter intercom. The kiosk display inset and camera coverage are invented cinematic devices, not documented Shannon surveillance capabilities. Nobody is identified as watching the assembled feeds yet.
- Timestamp jumps cover the drive and the freight wait without conventional scenic shots. No camera follows him through a wall or reads an unshown private thought.
- The annotation in Scene 6 automatically adds the battery, protective case and charger to Scene 4. The rigid case is ordinary travel protection; specialist freight packaging is supplied later.
- The next-day carrier acceptance, €1,850 charge and three-to-five-working-day estimate are fictional outcomes, not transport promises.
- The board, mast and foil have **not** been added to his luggage. The author specified the battery; whether he already has a compatible board in Miami, obtains one there or uses the battery differently remains open for later scenes.
- These scenes end at the booking stage. They do not yet establish his arrival in Miami or the battery’s customs release.
