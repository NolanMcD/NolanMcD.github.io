Miami rain usually comes with a familiar question: how much of the afternoon am I about to lose?

But a September 14 post from NWS Miami caught my attention for a different reason. It described a **non-tropical upper-level low approaching from the northeast**, potentially bringing a more active pattern than the daily cycle of showers and storms.

That sounded a little like winter weather mechanics showing up before Miami had finished sweating through summer. So I asked: **is this a winter-type system arriving early because of El Niño?**

The useful answer is: the resemblance makes sense, but the cause needs more care.

El Niño matters for Florida's seasonal weather, especially in winter, but that does not establish that it caused this particular September system.</p></aside>

## The weather machinery

Three terms do most of the work here.

**Upper-level low:** a circulation of relatively low pressure high in the atmosphere. The word *low* does not automatically mean a tropical storm or hurricane. NWS explicitly described this one as non-tropical.

**Rex block:** a blocking pattern with a high to the north and a low to the south. That arrangement can interrupt the usual west-to-east progression of weather systems. The important detail is their position relative to each other; the name does not tell you how hard it will rain in your neighborhood. [NWS's blocking-pattern explainer](https://www.weather.gov/source/zhu/ZHU_Training_Page/Miscellaneous/longwave/longwave_patterns.html) describes the configuration.

**Cutoff low:** an upper-level low that separates from the main westerly flow. Instead of being carried along in the usual current, it can move independently. That helps explain why the track and timing deserve attention, not just the presence of an L on a map.

In the September 14 discussion, NWS Miami described a ridge over the central U.S. and Eastern Seaboard, with an upper low developing offshore in the western Atlantic and moving southwest underneath it toward Florida. Forecasters expected the low to become cut off around Thursday and Friday, September 17–18. They also described increasing northeast winds and support for rising air in a moist atmosphere. That combination favors more widespread showers and thunderstorms. [NWS Miami forecast discussion, September 14](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=MFL&issuedby=MFL&product=AFD).

## The winter comparison

Florida's warm-season thunderstorms often involve heat, moisture, and sea-breeze boundaries. The location of the subtropical ridge and the background wind help determine where storms form and move. This is a recurring daily rhythm, although tropical disturbances and other weather systems can interrupt it. [NWS Florida thunderstorm-season overview](https://www.weather.gov/tbw/RainySeason).

A larger upper-air disturbance changes the emphasis. Instead of focusing only on where today's sea breezes collide, the question becomes how a broader circulation affects lift, moisture, and wind over several days.

That is why *winter-like* felt like a useful comparison to me. Florida's cool-season weather often puts more emphasis on the jet stream and passing disturbances. It is a comparison of **weather mechanisms**, not evidence that winter arrived early.

| --- | --- | --- |
| What organizes the weather? | Daily heating and sea-breeze interactions | A larger upper-level circulation and blocking pattern |
| What deserves attention? | Where and when storms develop each day | The low's track, timing, and interaction with moisture |

The term *cold-core low* can also be misleading at a glance. It describes the system's temperature structure, not a promise of sweater weather at street level. In fact, the same NWS discussion still forecast highs in the upper 80s to low 90s. It noted that colder air aloft could favor instability, while dry air might inhibit deeper convection. Those competing ingredients were part of the uncertainty, not a declaration that severe weather was inevitable. [NWS Miami discussion](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=MFL&issuedby=MFL&product=AFD).

## Where El Niño fits
{: #where-el-nino-fits }

The El Niño part of my question was not coming from nowhere. In its **September 10, 2026** advisory, NOAA's Climate Prediction Center reported strengthening El Niño conditions, a Niño-3.4 temperature anomaly of **+1.8°C**, and a **greater than 90% chance of a very strong event during fall and winter 2026–27**. Those are dated observations and seasonal probabilities, not a forecast for this individual low. [CPC ENSO Diagnostic Discussion](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/ensodisc.shtml).

El Niño can influence the jet stream, shifting the probabilities of weather patterns far from the tropical Pacific. For the United States, its influence is strongest in winter. Florida is among the places where wetter cool-season conditions are a familiar association. [NOAA's ENSO FAQ](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/understanding-climate/el-nino-and-la-nina-frequently-asked-questions).

But those connections do not let me work backward from one unusual forecast and stamp **caused by El Niño** on it. The local NWS discussion explained this system through the ridge, low, and jet dynamics; it did not attribute the event to ENSO.

I started with a question about whether a seasonal climate pattern had brought winter forward. I ended up with a more useful distinction: **a climate pattern can change the odds without being a demonstrated explanation for every event that fits the pattern.**

## What I'm taking away

The interesting part of this forecast is the machinery. An approaching upper low and blocking ridge offer a different way to organize South Florida's weather from the daily pattern I am used to watching.

I also want to keep the confidence levels straight:

- **In the forecast:** an approaching non-tropical upper low and a change toward more active weather.
- **Useful background:** El Niño can influence Florida's cool-season storm track.
- **Not established here:** that El Niño caused this specific system, or that September is behaving like winter in every respect.

For day-to-day plans, I would go back to the [current NWS Miami forecast and briefings](https://www.weather.gov/mfl/briefing), especially before heading onto the water. A September 14 explanation should not become an accidental forecast for someone reading this weeks later.
